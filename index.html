0.1 Trigonometry and the Unit Circle

📖 Why Trigonometry Matters

Opening analogy: Imagine a Ferris wheel. Standing to the side, you see cabins moving up and down. Sound waves work the same way—they oscillate back and forth. Trigonometry is the language for describing those oscillations.

Every sound is fundamentally a sine wave. Well, complex sounds are combinations, but first you need to understand a pure sine wave.

What you'll learn:

  • What sine and cosine actually are (coordinates on a circle, not ratios)
  • Why we use radians instead of degrees in acoustics
  • What phase means in sound
  • The unit circle as your mental model

🔧 The Unit Circle

A circle with radius 1. Any point on it has coordinates (x, y) where:

x = cos(angle)
y = sin(angle)
tan(angle) = y/x

Key angles:

AngleRadianssincos
0°001
90°π/210
180°π0-1
270°3π/2-10

📖 Radians vs Degrees

Degrees (360° in a circle) are convenient for everyday angles. But calculus and physics love radians because the mathematics is cleaner. The derivative of sin(x) is cos(x) only if x is in radians.

1 radian ≈ 57.3°
Full circle = 2π radians = 360°

📖 Phase: The Position of a Wave

Phase tells you where in its cycle a wave is at any moment. If two loudspeakers play the same tone but the sound from one arrives slightly later (due to distance), the waves have a phase difference. Your brain uses this to figure out which speaker is closer.

y(t) = sin(2πft + φ)

Here φ (phi) is the phase offset.

0.2 Superposition — Adding Waves (CRITICAL CONCEPT)

📖 The Principle of Superposition

Opening analogy: Drop pebbles into water. Ripples overlap. Where they meet, the height is the sum of the individual ripples. Waves add.

Superposition: When waves overlap, the total displacement is the sum of individual displacements.

This explains:

  • Interference (waves making each other louder or quieter)
  • Standing waves in rooms
  • Beats (wobbling sound when frequencies are close)
  • Harmonics and timbre
  • All of Fourier analysis

Understand superposition, understand most of acoustics.

🔧 Constructive & Destructive Interference

Constructive: Waves in phase (same timing) add up to something bigger:

Wave 1: y₁ = A sin(2πft)
Wave 2: y₂ = A sin(2πft)
Sum: 2A sin(2πft) → LOUDER

Destructive: Waves out of phase (opposite timing) cancel:

Wave 1: y₁ = A sin(2πft)
Wave 2: y₂ = A sin(2πft + π) = -A sin(2πft)
Sum: 0 → SILENCE

This is how noise-cancelling headphones work.

📖 Beats

When two frequencies are slightly different, you hear wobbling. This is a beat.

f_beat = |f₁ - f₂|

Example: 440 Hz + 442 Hz = 2 Hz beat (wobbles twice per second). Musicians use beats to tune.

🔧 Create Beats in Python

import numpy as np
from scipy.io import wavfile

sr = 44100  # sample rate
dur = 5     # seconds
t = np.linspace(0, dur, int(sr * dur))

# Two close frequencies
f1, f2 = 440, 442
wave1 = 0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f1 * t)
wave2 = 0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f2 * t)
combined = wave1 + wave2

# Save
wavfile.write('beats.wav', sr, (combined * 32767).astype(np.int16))
print(f"Beat freq = {f2 - f1} Hz")

Open in Audacity to see the amplitude wobbling.

0.3 Algebra Tools for Acoustics

📖 Rearranging Equations

Key skill: whatever you do to one side, do to the other.

Example: c = f λ

  • Want λ? Divide both sides by f: λ = c/f
  • Want f? Divide both sides by λ: f = c/λ

📖 Scientific Notation

Acoustics spans huge ranges. Instead of writing 0.0000001, write 1 × 10⁻⁷.

0.0000001 = 1 × 10⁻⁷
100,000 = 1 × 10⁵
10ᵃ × 10ᵇ = 10^(a+b)
10ᵃ / 10ᵇ = 10^(a-b)

📖 Units

Every number needs units. Convert by multiplying by fractions that equal 1.

Example: 5 km to metres:

5 km × (1000 m / 1 km) = 5000 m

The Physics of Sound

Weeks 7-14 • What sound is and how to measure it

▼
1.1 What Sound Actually Is

📖 Longitudinal Waves and Pressure

Opening analogy: A line of people standing together. One pushes forward, then back. The disturbance propagates down the line. Each person doesn't travel far—just jiggles back and forth. That's sound.

Sound is a longitudinal wave—disturbance parallel to propagation direction. Air molecules jiggle back and forth. The jiggling propagates outward as a pressure wave.

Light vs Sound:

  • Light: transverse (oscillates perpendicular to travel), no medium needed
  • Sound: longitudinal (oscillates parallel to travel), needs a medium

📖 Speed of Sound

Depends on stiffness (resists compression) and density:

c = √(stiffness / density)

Common speeds:

  • Air (20°C): ~343 m/s
  • Water: ~1480 m/s (5× faster)
  • Steel: ~5960 m/s (very stiff)
  • Foam: ~500 m/s (soft, squishy)

Temperature dependence: c(T) ≈ 331 + 0.6 × T_celsius

🔧 Quick Calculations

Example 1: Sound travels 30 m to a wall. How long for the echo?

time = distance / speed = 30 / 343 ≈ 0.087 s

Example 2: 40 kHz ultrasound. Wavelength?

λ = c / f = 343 / 40000 ≈ 0.0086 m = 8.6 mm

📝 Homework

Q1: Why does sound pass through a window but light gets reflected by fog?

Q2: Sound underwater travels 1480 m/s. How long to travel 100 m? Compare to air.

Q3: You're 100 m from a cliff. You clap. How long until echo?

1.2 Acoustic Quantities: Frequency, Wavelength, Amplitude, c = fλ

📖 Frequency

Number of cycles per second. Unit: Hertz (Hz).

  • 20 Hz: subsonic vibration
  • 100 Hz: deep bass
  • 440 Hz: A note (musical reference)
  • 1000 Hz: treble, easy to hear
  • 20000 Hz: upper limit of human hearing
  • 40000 Hz: ultrasound

You perceive frequency as pitch.

📖 Wavelength

Distance from one peak to the next. Symbol: λ.

20 Hz: λ ≈ 17 m (huge!)
100 Hz: λ ≈ 3.4 m
1000 Hz: λ ≈ 34 cm
10000 Hz: λ ≈ 34 mm

Why it matters: Room modes (frequencies matching room size resonate). Loudspeaker design (cones must move ~λ distance). Interference patterns.

📖 Amplitude

Maximum displacement from rest. Measured in Pascals (Pa).

  • Threshold of hearing: 20 μPa
  • Normal conversation: ~0.02 Pa
  • Loud music: ~0.2 Pa
  • Jet engine: ~200 Pa
  • Threshold of pain: ~20 Pa

10-million-fold range! This is why we use logarithmic scales (decibels).

📖 The Wave Equation: c = fλ (THE Formula)

c = f λ

In any medium, speed is constant. High frequency → short wavelength. Low frequency → long wavelength.

Worked example: 256 Hz (middle C). Wavelength?

λ = c / f = 343 / 256 ≈ 1.34 m

Phase difference example: Two mics 17 cm apart, measuring 1000 Hz. Phase difference?

λ = 343 / 1000 = 0.343 m. Fraction: 0.17 / 0.343 ≈ 0.5. Phase: 0.5 × 2π = π (180° out of phase)

🔧 Intensity Formula

Power per area. Relates pressure to intensity:

I = p² / (ρc)

For air: ρc ≈ 410 kg/(m²s).

Example: 0.01 Pa pressure. Intensity?

I = (0.01)² / 410 ≈ 2.4 × 10⁻⁷ W/m²

1.3 Decibels and Sound Pressure Level (SPL)

📖 Why Logarithms (Human Ears Respond to Ratios)

Your ear doesn't perceive linearly. Doubling pressure = barely noticeable. Need 10× pressure for roughly "twice as loud."

Range: threshold of hearing (20 μPa) to pain (20 Pa) = millionfold! Logarithms compress this.

Decibel formula:

L_p = 20 log₁₀(p / p_ref)

p_ref = 20 μPa (threshold of hearing) = 0 dB SPL.

🔧 Calculating dB

Example 1: Threshold of hearing: p = 20 μPa

L = 20 log₁₀(20×10⁻⁶ / 20×10⁻⁶) = 20 log₁₀(1) = 0 dB ✓

Example 2: Normal conversation ≈ 60 dB. What pressure?

60 = 20 log₁₀(p / 20×10⁻⁶)
3 = log₁₀(p / 20×10⁻⁶)
p = 1000 × 20×10⁻⁶ = 0.02 Pa

Rule of thumb: Doubling pressure = +6 dB

Example 3: Speaker at 94 dB. Move half the distance (intensity quadruples = pressure doubles). New SPL = 94 + 6 = 100 dB.

📖 SPL Reference Values

SoundSPLPressure
Threshold0 dB20 μPa

Traffic70 dB0.063 Pa
Lawn mower90 dB0.63 Pa

Every 20 dB = 10× pressure.

📖 A-Weighting

Human ears aren't equally sensitive to all frequencies. Sensitive around 2-4 kHz. Less sensitive to very low and very high frequencies.

A-weighted decibels (dB A) match human perception. 50 Hz at 70 dB SPL might be 50 dB A (sounds much quieter).

Use dB A when: Environmental noise, anything where perception matters.

Use dB SPL when: Acoustic engineering, specific frequencies.

1.4 Your First Measurement — RT60 (Reverb Time)

📖 What is RT60?

Time for sound to decay 60 dB after source stops (drops to 1-millionth intensity).

  • Dead room: RT60 ≈ 0.3 s (studio with foam)
  • Living room: RT60 ≈ 0.5-1.0 s
  • Concert hall: RT60 ≈ 1.5-2.5 s
  • Cathedral: RT60 ≈ 3-5+ s

Why it matters: Speech clarity, music character, acoustic design. Varies with frequency.

📖 Sabine Equation

RT60 = 0.161 × V / A

V = room volume (m³). A = total absorption (sabins). 1 sabin = absorption of 1 m² perfectly absorbing surface.

Worked example: Studio 4×5×3 m, panels α = 0.8.

V = 60 m³. Area = 40 + 30 + 24 = 94 m². A = 94 × 0.8 = 75.2 sabins.

RT60 = 0.161 × 60 / 75.2 ≈ 0.13 s (very dead)

🔧 Measure RT60 with REW

Step 1: Download REW from roomeqwizard.com. Install.

Step 2: Open REW. Measure → Microphone and Speaker Setup. Select inputs/outputs.

Step 3: Tools → Generate Sweep. 20 Hz to 20 kHz, 10-30 s duration.

Step 4: Play sweep. REW records decay after it ends.

Step 5: Tools → Impulse Response. REW calculates RT60 at different frequencies.

Interpret: If RT60 varies a lot, room has uneven absorption. Bass longer than treble? Need more bass treatment.

Practical: If you have REW, measure your room. Record RT60 at 125, 500, 1000, 4000 Hz. Describe acoustic character (boomy? dead?)
