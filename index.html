0.1 Trigonometry and the Unit Circle

🎯 Opening Hook: Why Circles Unlock Waves

You already understand circles. A steering wheel spinning. A clock face ticking. The horizon curving. The rings in water when you throw a pebble. In this module, we're going to use a very simple circle—one with radius 1—as a tool to understand waves. Not because it's magic, but because it naturally explains how things that go round and round (like rotating wheels or oscillating motion) can also describe things that go up and down.

Here's the key insight: sound is rotation viewed from the side. Imagine a wheel spinning. If you stand to the side and watch a single point on the wheel, you see it moving up and down. That up-and-down motion is a sine wave. Sound waves work exactly the same way. This module builds that intuition.

🔍 Part 1: What is a Circle?

A circle is every point equidistant from a centre point. That distance is the radius. If you walk around a circle of radius 1, you're always exactly 1 unit away from the middle.

Picture this: You're standing in the middle of a perfect circle. A friend walks around it. At any moment, no matter where they are—north, east, southwest, whatever—they're always the same distance away. That's what a circle is. The distance they stay from you is the radius.

Now here's why circles matter: circles describe rotation. And rotation describes oscillation. Waves oscillate. This is why circles unlock wave behaviour. When something rotates, different observers see different things. Someone rotating sees circular motion. Someone standing to the side sees up-and-down motion. Both are true. They're just different perspectives on the same thing.

🎯 Part 2: The Unit Circle (Why We Use It)

We use a circle with radius 1—the unit circle—not because radius 1 is special, but because the maths works out elegantly. When the radius is 1, a lot of things simplify. Radius 1 means every point on the circle is exactly 1 unit from the centre.

Imagine standing at the centre of this circle, looking right (towards 3 o'clock on a clock face, or the direction "east"). A friend starts there and walks around the circle. As they walk, their position changes. We can describe that position in two ways:

By coordinates: "You're 0.707 units right and 0.707 units up" (these are x and y).

By angle: "You've rotated 45° (or π/4 radians) from the starting point."

Both describe the same position. Here's the magic: on the unit circle, those coordinate numbers ARE sine and cosine. The x-coordinate is cosine. The y-coordinate is sine. This isn't a separate thing—the coordinates of the circle ARE the sine and cosine values.

🔧 Part 3: Sine and Cosine Are Coordinates (Not Ratios)

This is the most important insight. Sine and cosine aren't mysterious ratios. They're just coordinates on the unit circle.

Here's how to think about it: Draw a unit circle. Pick any point on it. From the centre, draw a line to that point. Now draw a vertical line straight down from your point to the horizontal axis. That forms a right triangle. The triangle's corners are: the centre, your point, and where the vertical line meets the horizontal.

In this triangle:

  • Cosine is the horizontal leg (how far left-right from the centre). It's the x-coordinate. On the unit circle, this is always between -1 and 1.
  • Sine is the vertical leg (how far up-down from the centre). It's the y-coordinate. Also between -1 and 1.
  • The hypotenuse is always 1 (the radius of the unit circle).

So when people say "sine of 45° equals 0.707", they mean "when you rotate 45° from the start, the y-coordinate (how far up) is 0.707." That's all it is. A coordinate.

🎨 Visual: The Unit Circle

Before we look at numbers, visualise this. Every point on this circle can be described by just two coordinates: how far right (cosine) and how far up (sine):

How to read this: Pick any angle. Draw a line from the centre to that angle on the circle. The x-coordinate (how far right) is cosine. The y-coordinate (how far up) is sine. The Pythagorean theorem guarantees: cos²(θ) + sin²(θ) = 1 always.

🎯 Key Angles: What Sine and Cosine Look Like

Let's trace what happens as your friend walks around the circle starting from 3 o'clock (0°):

Angle (degrees) Angle (radians) x-coordinate (cos) y-coordinate (sin) Where on circle?
0° 0 1 0 Right (3 o'clock)
90° π/2 0 1 Top (12 o'clock)
180° π -1 0 Left (9 o'clock)
270° 3π/2 0 -1 Bottom (6 o'clock)
45° π/4 0.707 0.707 Northeast

Notice the pattern: at the 4 cardinal points, sine and cosine are either 0, 1, or -1. At 45°, they're both the same. This pattern repeats and is the foundation of trigonometry.

🎯 Part 4: Why Radians? (Not Just "Degrees Are Bad")

Degrees are arbitrary—humans decided 360 because of ancient Babylonian calendar maths, not because of anything natural. Radians are different. One radian is defined as the angle where the arc length equals the radius.

On the unit circle (radius = 1), the arc length in units equals the angle in radians. Walk 1 unit around the circle? You've rotated 1 radian. Walk π units around the circle? You've rotated π radians. This is natural and elegant.

Why does this matter? Because the derivative of sin(x) in degrees is ugly and involves extra constants. The derivative of sin(x) in radians is cos(x)—clean and simple. Physics and acoustics use radians because the mathematics is cleaner. All calculus prefers radians.

Quick conversion: 360° = 2π radians. To convert from degrees: radians = degrees × (π/180). To convert to degrees: degrees = radians × (180/π).

360° = 2π radians ≈ 6.28 radians
180° = π radians ≈ 3.14 radians
90° = π/2 radians ≈ 1.57 radians

🎯 Part 5: Phase (What it Really Means)

Phase is just "where on the circle are you starting?" If a wave starts at the top of the circle (90° or π/2 radians), it has a different phase than one starting at the right (0°). Same frequency, different starting point.

Clock dancer analogy: Two dancers dancing to the same music, but one started their routine 2 beats later. They're doing the same moves, just at different moments. The one who started late is "out of phase." Their motion is shifted in time, which looks like it's shifted on the circle.

In the equation y(t) = sin(2πft + φ), the φ (phi) is the phase offset—where you start on the circle. If φ = 0, you start at 0° (right side). If φ = π/2, you start at 90° (top). If φ = π, you start at 180° (left side)—inverted from the first case. This is why phase matters so much in acoustics: two speakers playing the same frequency might have different phases, and that determines whether they constructively reinforce each other or destructively cancel.

🔧 Now Code It: Draw the Unit Circle

Now that you understand conceptually, let's make a computer draw it for us. This is where Python comes in.

A note on the code below: If you haven't seen Python before, don't worry! The code below is just instructions we're giving to the computer: "Create 100 angles... calculate their coordinates... draw them as a circle... add labels." Each line is a small instruction. In Module 0.2, you'll learn how to install Python and run code like this. For now, just understand what this code is doing (the comments explain each line).

This code creates a visual representation of exactly what we've been discussing:

# ========== DRAWING THE UNIT CIRCLE IN PYTHON ==========
# This code creates a visual representation of the unit circle
# showing how sine and cosine give us the y and x coordinates

# Import libraries: matplotlib for drawing, numpy for math
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt  # pyplot: "plot" library for creating graphs/visualizations
import numpy as np  # numpy: "numerical python" - handles arrays and math operations like sin/cos

# STEP 1: CREATE 100 ANGLES AROUND THE CIRCLE
# ============================================
# We want to draw a smooth circle. To do that, we need lots of points.
# np.linspace(start, end, number_of_points) creates evenly-spaced numbers
# 0 to 2π (which equals 360°) with 100 points between them
# Think of it as: "Give me 100 angles spread evenly around a full circle"
theta = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 100)

# STEP 2: CALCULATE X AND Y COORDINATES FOR EACH ANGLE
# ===================================================
# Remember: on the unit circle, x = cos(θ) and y = sin(θ)
# We're going to apply the cosine function to all 100 angles at once
# This gives us 100 x-coordinates (one for each angle)
x = np.cos(theta)  # x = cosine of each angle (how far right/left)
y = np.sin(theta)  # y = sine of each angle (how far up/down)
# Now we have two lists: one with 100 x-values, one with 100 y-values

# STEP 3: CREATE A BLANK FIGURE TO DRAW ON
# ========================================
# plt.figure() creates a new blank drawing surface
# figsize=(8, 8) means the drawing will be 8 inches by 8 inches (square)
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 8))

# STEP 4: PLOT THE CIRCLE
# =======================
# plt.plot(x, y, ...) draws a line connecting all the points
# 'b-' means: 'b' = blue color, '-' = solid line
# linewidth=2 makes the line 2 pixels thick (thicker = easier to see)
# label='Unit Circle' names this line (used in the legend)
plt.plot(x, y, 'b-', linewidth=2, label='Unit Circle')

# STEP 5: MARK THE CENTRE POINT
# =============================
# The unit circle is centered at (0, 0) - the origin
# 'ko' means: 'k' = black, 'o' = circle marker
# markersize=8 makes the dot 8 pixels across
plt.plot(0, 0, 'ko', markersize=8, label='Centre')

# STEP 6: MARK THE FOUR CARDINAL POINTS
# =====================================
# These are the special points: 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°
# At 0°:   (1, 0)  - far right, same height as center - red dot
# At 90°:  (0, 1)  - same distance from center, straight up - green dot
# At 180°: (-1, 0) - far left, same height as center - cyan dot
# At 270°: (0, -1) - same distance from center, straight down - magenta dot

plt.plot(0, 1, 'go', markersize=8)    # 'g' = green   (90° point)

plt.plot(0, -1, 'mo', markersize=8)   # 'm' = magenta (270° point)

# STEP 7: SHOW A SPECIFIC ANGLE EXAMPLE (45°)
# ===========================================
# Let's mark the 45° angle to show how it works
# π/4 radians = 45 degrees (converting from our formula: π/4 = π/4)
angle_45 = np.pi/4

# Draw a red dashed line from the center to the 45° point
# [0, np.cos(angle_45)] means: x-coordinates from 0 to cos(45°)
# [0, np.sin(angle_45)] means: y-coordinates from 0 to sin(45°)
# This creates a line showing the radius at 45°

# Mark the 45° point with a red star
# At 45°: cos(45°) ≈ 0.707 and sin(45°) ≈ 0.707
# (the coordinates are equal at 45° because it's a 45° angle!)

# STEP 8: FORMAT THE GRAPH
# =======================
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)      # Add a background grid (alpha=0.3 makes it faint)
plt.axis('equal')              # Make x and y scales equal (so the circle looks round)
plt.axhline(y=0, color='k', linewidth=0.5)  # Draw the horizontal axis (y=0 line)
plt.axvline(x=0, color='k', linewidth=0.5)  # Draw the vertical axis (x=0 line)

# These labels tell us what the axes represent

plt.title('The Unit Circle: Sine and Cosine as Coordinates', fontsize=14)

plt.legend()                   # Display the legend (labels we defined earlier)

plt.ylim(-1.5, 1.5)            # Set y-axis to show from -1.5 to +1.5

# ===========================
# This command opens a window and shows your drawing

🎯 Part 6: Why Is Sine at 90° Equal to 1? (Understanding the Values)

This is the kind of question that deepens understanding. Let's reason through it.

At 90°, you're pointing straight up. Your friend is at the top of the circle. The y-coordinate of straight up is maximum—they're as far up as they can be. Since the radius is 1 (one unit away from the centre), and they're pointing straight up, the y-coordinate is 1. Hence sin(90°) = 1.

At 0°, you're pointing right. Your friend is at the rightmost point. The y-coordinate is zero because they're not up or down at all—they're on the horizontal axis. Hence sin(0°) = 0.

At 180°, you're pointing left. Your friend is at the leftmost point. The y-coordinate is 0 again (they're on the horizontal axis, just on the opposite side). Sin(180°) = 0.

At 270°, you're pointing down. The y-coordinate is -1 (maximum downward, opposite of 90°). Sin(270°) = -1.

Once you understand this—that sine is the y-coordinate and the y-axis ranges from -1 to 1 on the unit circle—all the sine values make sense. They're not arbitrary. They're geometric.

🔬 Deep Dive: Why Cosine Lags Behind Sine (The Phase Shift)

If you look at the graph above, cosine is always 90° (π/2 radians) ahead of sine. Sine starts at 0, peaks at 90°. Cosine starts at 1, reaches 0 at 90°. Why?

Picture the circle. Start at 0°. At that moment, sine (y-coordinate) is 0, and cosine (x-coordinate) is 1. As you rotate, the y-coordinate grows faster than the x-coordinate shrinks (geometrically). By 90°, you've swapped: y is at maximum (1), x is at 0. By 180°, both are back where they started, just mirrored. This creates the shifted wave pattern.

Mathematically: sin(θ) = cos(θ - π/2). Cosine is just sine shifted by 90°. Or equivalently, sin(θ + π/2) = cos(θ). This phase shift matters enormously in acoustics. Two speakers playing the same frequency with a 90° phase difference create interesting interference patterns.

0.2 Superposition — Adding Waves (CRITICAL CONCEPT)

🎯 Opening: Why This Is The Most Important Concept in Acoustics

Drop pebbles into water. Ripples spread outward. Where two ripples meet, something interesting happens. If they're aligned (crest meets crest), the water rises higher. If they're misaligned (crest meets trough), they flatten out. This is superposition, and it explains almost everything interesting about sound.

Superposition: When waves overlap, the total displacement is the sum of individual displacements.

This simple rule explains:

  • Why two speakers can cancel each other (noise-cancelling headphones)
  • Why two speakers can reinforce each other (surround sound)
  • Why rooms have dead zones where music sounds quiet
  • Why beats happen when two pitches are close
  • Why harmonics create timbre (why a piano sounds different from a trumpet)
  • Why Fourier analysis works (all complex sounds are just sine waves added together)

Master superposition, and you understand most of acoustics.

🔍 Part 1: What Happens When Two Waves Overlap?

Imagine two speakers side by side, both playing the same 440 Hz tone (A note). Each is creating a pressure wave. Sound travels at 343 m/s. When the waves reach a point in the room, they add together. The key: they don't always add the same way.

Case 1: In Phase (Same Timing)

If the speakers are in phase—crests from speaker A arrive at the same time as crests from speaker B—the crests add. You get bigger crests. The troughs also add (trough + trough = bigger trough). The total wave has double the amplitude.

Wave 1: y₁ = A sin(2πft)
Wave 2: y₂ = A sin(2πft) [same phase, phase difference = 0]
Sum: y_total = 2A sin(2πft) → LOUDER (twice the amplitude)

Case 2: Out of Phase (Opposite Timing)

If the speakers are 180° out of phase (π radians), crests from speaker A arrive when troughs arrive from speaker B. A crest and a trough cancel. The total is silence.

Wave 1: y₁ = A sin(2πft)
Wave 2: y₂ = A sin(2πft + π) = -A sin(2πft) [opposite phase, phase difference = π]
Sum: y_total = 0 → SILENCE (complete cancellation)

This is the principle behind noise-cancelling headphones: they measure the incoming noise and generate an inverted copy that cancels it out.

Case 3: Partial Phase Difference

Most of the time, the phase difference is between 0 and π. The crests and troughs partially align. You get something in between—louder than one speaker alone, but not as loud as if they were perfectly in phase. The amplitude depends on the phase difference.

🎯 Part 2: Understanding Constructive and Destructive Interference

Constructive interference: The waves amplify each other. Crests meet crests. This happens when the phase difference is 0°, 360°, 720°, etc. (multiples of 360°, or multiples of 2π radians).

Destructive interference: The waves cancel each other. Crests meet troughs. This happens when the phase difference is 180°, 540°, 900°, etc. (odd multiples of 180°, or odd multiples of π radians).

The phase difference between two waves depends on where you measure them. Two speakers 0.5 m apart playing 440 Hz won't be in phase everywhere in the room. At some distances, they reinforce. At others, they cancel. This is why room acoustics is so complex—reflections create overlapping waves at different phases throughout the space.

🔍 Part 3: Beats — Superposition of Close Frequencies

What if two waves have slightly different frequencies? They start in phase, then gradually go out of phase, then back in phase. This creates a wobbling effect called beats.

When you play 440 Hz from one speaker and 442 Hz from another, you hear a single tone that gets louder and quieter twice per second. That wobbling is the beat. It's the interference pattern between the two frequencies.

f_beat = |f₁ - f₂|

In this case, f_beat = 442 - 440 = 2 Hz. The sound gets louder and quieter 2 times per second. If you played 440 Hz and 445 Hz, f_beat would be 5 Hz (faster wobbling). Musicians use this to tune instruments: they adjust until the beat frequency disappears (two notes are the same frequency).

Why does this happen? The two waves are represented as:

y₁ = A sin(2πf₁t)
y₂ = A sin(2πf₂t)
y_total = A[sin(2πf₁t) + sin(2πf₂t)]

When f₁ ≈ f₂, the sum creates an amplitude envelope—a slowly varying amplitude modulation on top of a carrier frequency. That modulation is the beat you hear.

Here's what happens when two waves meet. The result depends entirely on their phase relationship:

Constructive Interference (Phase difference = 0°)

Crests meet crests, troughs meet troughs → AMPLIFIED (twice as loud)

Destructive Interference (Phase difference = 180°)

Crests meet troughs → CANCELLATION (silence!)

Beats (Phase difference changes over time)

When two waves have slightly different frequencies, they drift in and out of phase → WOBBLING (gets louder and quieter)

🔧 Create Beats in Python

Now let's generate these interference patterns with Python code and listen to them. The code below does this:

  1. Set up the timing: We'll create 5 seconds of audio at 44.1 kHz (44,100 samples per second)
  2. Create two waves: 440 Hz and 442 Hz (slightly different frequencies)
  3. Add them together: This is superposition—the two waves interfere
  4. Save the result: Write it as a .wav file you can listen to
  5. Plot it: Show what the interference looks like visually

Don't worry about every line of code right now. Focus on the overall logic: create waves → add them → save the result. If this is your first time seeing Python, Module 0.2 will teach you step-by-step.

# ========== CREATING BEAT INTERFERENCE IN PYTHON ==========
# This code generates two slightly different frequencies,
# combines them, and creates an audio file you can listen to
# You'll hear the wobbling beat effect in action!

import numpy as np                  # For math: sin, arrays
from scipy.io import wavfile        # For saving .wav audio files
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt     # For plotting the waveform

# ============ SECTION 1: SET UP THE AUDIO PARAMETERS ============
# These parameters define how we'll create the audio

sr = 44100  # Sample rate: 44,100 samples per second
            # This is CD-quality audio. Each second of sound has 44,100 discrete values
            # Higher sample rate = more accurate representation of the sound

duration = 5  # Create 5 seconds of audio
              # You'll hear about 10 beats in this time (2 Hz × 5 seconds)

# Create a time array: "at what times do we generate samples?"
# np.linspace(0, 5, 220500) creates 220,500 evenly-spaced times
# from 0 to 5 seconds (44,100 samples/second × 5 seconds = 220,500 total samples)
t = np.linspace(0, duration, int(sr * duration))

# ============ SECTION 2: DEFINE THE FREQUENCIES ============
# These are the two frequencies that will interfere

f1 = 440  # First frequency: 440 Hz (the musical note A)
          # This is the standard pitch used to tune pianos and orchestras

f2 = 442  # Second frequency: 442 Hz (2 Hz higher)
          # Only 2 Hz difference, so we'll hear them as one tone with wobbling

# Beat frequency will be: |f2 - f1| = |442 - 440| = 2 Hz
# This means we'll hear the sound get louder and quieter twice per second

# ============ SECTION 3: GENERATE THE TWO WAVES ============
# Now we create the actual waveforms using the sine function

# wave1 = 0.3 * sin(2π × 440 × t)
# Let's break this down:
# - The 0.3 is the amplitude (volume). 0.3 means 30% of maximum volume
# - 2π converts from frequency (Hz) to angular velocity (radians/second)
# - f1 is our frequency (440)
# - t is the time array (all 220,500 time points)
# - sin() creates the oscillation
# The result is a 440 Hz sine wave at 30% volume
wave1 = 0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f1 * t)

# wave2 = 0.3 * sin(2π × 442 × t)
# Same process, but with f2 = 442 Hz
# This wave oscillates slightly faster than wave1
# They start in sync, but gradually drift out of sync, then back in sync
# This drift is what creates the beat!
wave2 = 0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f2 * t)

# ============ SECTION 4: ADD THE WAVES (SUPERPOSITION) ============
# This is where the magic happens: we add the two waves together
# According to superposition rule: total displacement = sum of individual displacements

combined = wave1 + wave2
# At each time point, the combined wave is the sum of wave1 and wave2
# When the waves are in phase (peaks align), combined amplitude = 0.6 (loud!)
# When out of phase (peak meets trough), combined amplitude ≈ 0 (quiet!)
# This creates the wobbling sound

# ============ SECTION 5: SAVE THE AUDIO FILE ============
# Now we save this to a .wav file so you can listen to it

wavfile.write('beats.wav',                                    # Filename
              sr,                                             # Sample rate (44,100)
              (combined * 32767).astype(np.int16))           # Audio data converted to 16-bit integer format
# Note: .wav files store audio as integers, not decimals
# We multiply by 32767 because 16-bit integers range from -32768 to +32767

# ============ SECTION 6: PRINT INFORMATION ============
# Let the user know what beat frequency to expect

print(f"Beat frequency: {f2 - f1} Hz")  # This will print "Beat frequency: 2 Hz"
print(f"Expected wobble: {f2 - f1} wobbles per second")  # "Expected wobble: 2 wobbles per second"

# ============ SECTION 7: VISUALIZE THE FIRST 0.5 SECONDS ============
# We'll plot the waveform so you can SEE the beat envelope

# How many samples make up 0.5 seconds?
# If we have 44,100 samples per second, then:
# 0.5 seconds × 44,100 = 22,050 samples
plot_samples = int(sr * 0.5)

# Create a figure (blank drawing area)
plt.figure(figsize=(12, 4))  # 12 inches wide, 4 inches tall

# Plot only the first 0.5 seconds (first 22,050 samples)
# t[:plot_samples] = time values for first 22,050 samples
# combined[:plot_samples] = amplitude values for first 22,050 samples
plt.plot(t[:plot_samples], combined[:plot_samples], linewidth=1)

# Add labels and title
plt.xlabel('Time (seconds)')
plt.ylabel('Amplitude')
plt.title('Beats: 440 Hz + 442 Hz (2 Hz beat frequency)')
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# Display the graph
plt.show()

# ============ WHAT YOU'LL OBSERVE ============
# When you run this and look at the graph:
# - You'll see a FAST oscillation (the carrier frequency: ~441 Hz average)
# - You'll see a SLOW envelope (the amplitude getting louder/quieter: 2 Hz)
# - The envelope looks like it's "modulating" the fast oscillation
# - This is called "amplitude modulation"
#
# When you listen to beats.wav:
# - You'll hear a steady tone (around 441 Hz)
# - But the loudness will wobble: loud... quiet... loud... quiet...
# - At 2 Hz, you'll experience this wobble twice per second
# - It sounds like someone is turning a volume knob on and off
# - This is how musicians tune instruments: adjust until beats disappear!

Save this as a Python script and run it. It creates a .wav file called "beats.wav". Listen to it—you'll hear a tone that wobbles in and out (quieter, louder, quieter, etc.) twice per second. Open beats.wav in Audacity and look at the waveform. You'll see the amplitude modulation (the envelope) clearly.

The graph shows the first half second. Notice how the wave amplitude rises and falls—that's the beat envelope. The rapid oscillations inside are the carrier frequency (around 441 Hz, the average of 440 and 442). The slow wobbling is the beat frequency (2 Hz).

🔧 Constructive vs. Destructive: Generate Examples

Now let's create three .wav files that clearly show the difference between constructive, destructive, and partial interference. You'll actually hear how different they are:

# ========== DEMONSTRATING PHASE INTERFERENCE ==========
# This code shows how phase difference affects wave interference
# We'll create three separate audio files so you can hear the difference!

import numpy as np              # Math functions
from scipy.io import wavfile    # Saving .wav files

# ============ AUDIO PARAMETERS ============
sr = 44100  # 44.1 kHz sample rate (CD quality)
duration = 2  # 2 seconds of audio
t = np.linspace(0, duration, int(sr * duration))

f = 440  # Both waves at the SAME frequency (440 Hz)
         # This is important: we're testing phase difference, not frequency difference

# ============ AMPLITUDE ============
A = 0.3  # Amplitude: volume level (30% of maximum)
         # Each individual wave has amplitude A
         # If constructive: combined amplitude = 2A (twice as loud!)
         # If destructive: combined amplitude = 0 (silent!)

# ============ THE BASE WAVE ============
# This represents the first speaker playing 440 Hz
wave1 = A * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * t)

# ================================================================
# CASE 1: CONSTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE (Phase difference = 0°)
# ================================================================
# Two waves with the SAME phase arrive together
# Peaks meet peaks, troughs meet troughs
# Result: AMPLIFICATION! The sound gets twice as loud

print("\n=== CASE 1: CONSTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE ===")
print("Phase difference = 0° (waves perfectly aligned)")

# wave2_constructive has the exact same phase as wave1
# No phase offset (the "+ 0" part is implicit)
wave2_constructive = A * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * t)  # Exact same as wave1

# Add them together
combined_constructive = wave1 + wave2_constructive
# Result: amplitude = A + A = 2A (twice as loud)

# Save to file
wavfile.write('constructive.wav', sr, (combined_constructive * 32767).astype(np.int16))
print("✓ Saved: constructive.wav (should sound LOUD and clear)")
print("  Expected amplitude: ~0.6 (since A + A = 0.3 + 0.3)")

# ================================================================
# CASE 2: DESTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE (Phase difference = 180° = π)
# ================================================================
# Two waves with OPPOSITE phases: one is inverted
# When peak from wave1 arrives, trough from wave2 arrives
# Result: CANCELLATION! They destroy each other, creating silence

print("\n=== CASE 2: DESTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE ===")
print("Phase difference = 180° (waves completely inverted)")

# wave2_destructive is shifted by π (180°)
# The "+ np.pi" adds a phase offset of π radians
# sin(x + π) = -sin(x), so this is just the inverted wave
wave2_destructive = A * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * t + np.pi)  # Offset by π

# Add them together
combined_destructive = wave1 + wave2_destructive
# Result: amplitude ≈ 0 (complete cancellation!)
# Mathematically: sin(x) + sin(x + π) = sin(x) - sin(x) = 0

# Save to file
wavfile.write('destructive.wav', sr, (combined_destructive * 32767).astype(np.int16))
print("✓ Saved: destructive.wav (should sound nearly SILENT)")
print("  Expected amplitude: ~0 (they cancel out)")

# ================================================================
# CASE 3: PARTIAL INTERFERENCE (Phase difference = 45° = π/4)
# ================================================================
# Two waves with a partial phase difference
# They're neither perfectly aligned nor perfectly inverted
# Result: SOMETHING IN BETWEEN (louder than silent, quieter than double)

print("\n=== CASE 3: PARTIAL INTERFERENCE ===")
print("Phase difference = 45° (partially aligned)")

# wave2_partial is shifted by π/4 (45° = 1/8 of a full circle)
# This creates partial alignment
wave2_partial = A * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * t + np.pi/4)  # Offset by π/4

# Add them together
combined_partial = wave1 + wave2_partial
# Result: amplitude is somewhere between 0 and 2A
# How much overlap depends on the phase difference
# At π/4, we get A * sqrt(2) ≈ 0.42 (about 70% of the constructive case)

# Save to file
wavfile.write('partial.wav', sr, (combined_partial * 32767).astype(np.int16))
print("✓ Saved: partial.wav (should sound medium-loud)")
print("  Expected amplitude: ~0.42 (between 0 and 0.6)")

# ================================================================
# VERIFICATION: MEASURE THE ACTUAL AMPLITUDES
# ================================================================
# Let's check that our theory matches reality!

print("\n=== AMPLITUDE MEASUREMENTS ===")

# np.abs() takes absolute value (converts negative values to positive)
# np.max() finds the largest value in the array
# This tells us the peak amplitude of each combined wave

amp_constructive = np.max(np.abs(combined_constructive))
amp_destructive = np.max(np.abs(combined_destructive))
amp_partial = np.max(np.abs(combined_partial))

print(f"\nConstructive interference:")
print(f"  Measured amplitude: {amp_constructive:.3f}")
print(f"  Expected (2A):      {2*A:.3f}")
print(f"  ✓ Match! (Two waves add completely)")

print(f"\nDestructive interference:")
print(f"  Measured amplitude: {amp_destructive:.6f}")
print(f"  Expected (0):       0.000000")
print(f"  ✓ Match! (Waves cancel completely)")

print(f"\nPartial interference (π/4 phase):")
print(f"  Measured amplitude: {amp_partial:.3f}")
print(f"  Expected (A√2):     {A*np.sqrt(2):.3f}")
print(f"  ✓ Match! (Partial cancellation/reinforcement)")

# ================================================================
# HOW TO USE THESE FILES
# ================================================================
# Now listen to all three files in order:
# 1. constructive.wav — This should sound LOUD, clear, undistorted
# 2. destructive.wav — This should sound SILENT (nearly inaudible)
# 3. partial.wav — This should sound in between
#
# This is your ears learning what phase differences sound like!
# This principle is used in:
# - Noise-cancelling headphones (creating destructive interference)
# - Surround sound systems (using phase differences for directional effects)
# - Room acoustics (managing reflections that can interfere)

This creates three files. The constructive one will sound noticeably louder (twice as loud). The destructive one will be nearly silent. The partial one will be somewhere in between. Listen to all three. This teaches your ears what phase differences sound like.

🎯 Part 4: Why Phase Matters in Real Rooms

In a concert hall, sound doesn't just travel directly from the speaker to your ear. It bounces off walls, ceiling, floor. Each reflection creates a new "copy" of the sound arriving at your ear at a slightly different time. That delay creates a phase difference.

A reflection from the back wall might arrive 0.05 seconds later than the direct sound. If you're listening to 1000 Hz, that's a phase shift of:

Phase shift = (delay in time) × (frequency in Hz) × (2π) = 0.05 × 1000 × 2π ≈ 314 radians ≈ π radians (after wrapping). Roughly opposite phase. That frequency gets partially cancelled by its reflection.

This is why some rooms sound "boomy" at certain frequencies and "thin" at others. The reflections create constructive and destructive interference at different frequencies. Room acoustic treatment aims to either absorb reflections (so they don't interfere) or reflect them in ways that create constructive interference (reinforcing the direct sound).

🔧 Verify the Beat Formula

Let's confirm mathematically why the beat frequency equals the difference between the two frequencies:

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Two frequencies
f1, f2 = 440, 444    # 4 Hz difference
t = np.linspace(0, 2, 4410)  # 2 seconds at 44.1 kHz

# Two waves
wave1 = np.sin(2 * np.pi * f1 * t)
wave2 = np.sin(2 * np.pi * f2 * t)
combined = wave1 + wave2

# The envelope of the combined wave oscillates at the beat frequency
envelope_frequency = abs(f2 - f1)
print(f"f1 = {f1} Hz")
print(f"f2 = {f2} Hz")
print(f"Predicted beat frequency = {f2 - f1} Hz")

# Plot a short window
window = int(2.5 * 4410 / 10)  # First 0.25 seconds
plt.figure(figsize=(14, 4))
plt.plot(t[:window], combined[:window], linewidth=0.5, alpha=0.7, label='Combined wave')
plt.plot(t[:window], np.abs(combined[:window]), 'r-', linewidth=2, label='Envelope (beat)')
plt.xlabel('Time (seconds)')
plt.ylabel('Amplitude')
plt.title(f'Beats from {f1} Hz + {f2} Hz (beat freq = {f2-f1} Hz)')
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.show()

Run this. You'll see the rapid oscillations (the 440 Hz and 444 Hz mixing) with a slower envelope overlaid in red. That red envelope oscillates at the beat frequency. Count how many times it goes from quiet to loud to quiet again. It should match the beat frequency (4 times per second in this example).

🔬 Deep Dive: Deriving the Beat Formula Mathematically

If you sum two sine waves with slightly different frequencies:

y = sin(2πf₁t) + sin(2πf₂t)

Using the sum-to-product identity (a useful trig identity):

sin(A) + sin(B) = 2 sin((A+B)/2) cos((A-B)/2)

Setting A = 2πf₁t and B = 2πf₂t:

y = 2 sin(π(f₁+f₂)t) cos(π(f₂-f₁)t)

The first part, sin(π(f₁+f₂)t), is a sine wave at the average frequency. The second part, cos(π(f₂-f₁)t), is the amplitude modulation (the envelope). It oscillates at frequency |f₂-f₁|. That's the beat frequency.

So the beat frequency = |f₂ - f₁|. Not derived from magic—it's pure algebra applied to sine waves.

0.3 Algebra Tools for Acoustics

🎯 Rearranging Equations: The Core Skill

Most of acoustics involves equations. You'll constantly need to solve for different variables. The rule is simple: whatever you do to one side of an equation, do to the other side. That's it.

Example: c = f λ (speed of sound = frequency × wavelength)

  • Want to find λ (wavelength)? Divide both sides by f: λ = c/f
  • Want to find f (frequency)? Divide both sides by λ: f = c/λ
  • Want to find c (speed)? It's already isolated: c = f λ

🔧 Common Rearrangements in Acoustics

Original Solve for f Solve for λ
c = f λ f = c / λ λ = c / f
I = P / A P = I × A A = P / I
P = F / A F = P × A

🎯 Scientific Notation: Handling Huge and Tiny Numbers

Acoustics spans enormous ranges. Sound intensity ranges from 10⁻¹² W/m² (threshold of hearing) to 1 W/m² (painful). Rather than writing 0.000000000001, we write 1 × 10⁻¹².

Quick refresher:

  • 0.0000001 = 1 × 10⁻⁷ (move decimal 7 places left)
  • 100,000 = 1 × 10⁵ (move decimal 5 places right)
  • 3.5 million = 3.5 × 10⁶
  • 0.00045 = 4.5 × 10⁻⁴

Multiplying: 10ᵃ × 10ᵇ = 10^(a+b)

Dividing: 10ᵃ / 10ᵇ = 10^(a-b)

Example: (2 × 10⁻⁵) × (3 × 10⁸) = 6 × 10³

🎯 Units: Every Number Needs Them

A number without units is meaningless. 343 could be metres per second (speed of sound), or centimetres per second (a very slow speed), or something else entirely. Always include units.

Converting units: Multiply by a conversion factor that equals 1.

Example: Convert 5 km to metres.

5 km × (1000 m / 1 km) = 5000 m

The km cancels, leaving m. The conversion factor (1000 m / 1 km) equals 1 (one kilometre is 1000 metres), so multiplying doesn't change the value, just the units.

Another example: Convert 343 m/s to km/h.

343 m/s × (3600 s / 1 h) × (1 km / 1000 m)
= (343 × 3600 / 1000) km/h
= 1234.8 km/h
