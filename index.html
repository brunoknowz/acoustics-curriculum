🧠 The Philosophy of This Curriculum

You've spent 30 years building intuition that most students never get. A PWM LED controller means you already understand frequency, duty cycle, and the idea that you can describe a repeating signal with numbers. A laser projector means you understand how timing and angles translate into shapes — that's closer to Fourier analysis than you might think. We're not starting from zero. We're naming things you already feel.

Every concept here is anchored to something you can hear, measure, or build. When the maths gets abstract, we'll ground it in a physical experiment. When an experiment confuses you, the maths will explain why. They feed each other.

Math Tangents are labelled clearly — they're short detours to pick up a specific tool (logs, complex numbers, a bit of calculus) exactly when you need it, not before. No front-loading theory you can't use yet.

"Learn enough to do the next thing. Do the thing. Understand why. Repeat."

Phase 0 · Weeks 1–5
Calibration & Wave Language
You already speak this language. Let's learn to write it down.
🎯 Goal: Confident with sine, cosine, and the idea of a periodic signal📐 Math level: GCSE → A-level trig
0.1 Angles, Circles, and the Sine Wave

Your PWM LED blinks at a frequency and has a duty cycle. A sine wave also has a frequency — but it's smooth. The unit circle is the bridge between the two ideas.

📖 Study
  • Angles in degrees vs radians — why radians exist
  • The unit circle: x = cos(θ), y = sin(θ)
  • Plotting sin(t) and cos(t) — frequency, amplitude, period
  • Phase shift: sin(t + φ) — starting partway around the circle
🔧 Hands-On
  • Python: plot sin(2πft) for several frequencies using matplotlib
  • Arduino: use analogWrite() driven by sin() to smoothly fade an LED
  • Change the phase offset in code — describe what happens to the LED
📝 Homework
  • Sketch one full cycle of sin(t) from memory. Label: amplitude, period, phase = 0
  • Generate a 440 Hz sine tone in Python. Play it. Describe what you hear.
  • Question: If your laser moves in X and Y with sine waves 90° apart, what shape does it draw?
🎬 Watch
  • 3Blue1Brown: "But what is a Fourier series?" — let the visuals settle in
  • The Coding Train: "Coding Challenge #125: Fourier Series"
🔀 Math Tangent — Radians

360° = 2π radians. They exist because on a unit circle, arc length equals the angle in radians. Every acoustics formula uses radians. Spend 30 minutes converting between the two. Formula: radians = degrees × (π/180).

0.2 Superposition — Adding Waves Together

The single most important idea in all of acoustics. Two waves in the same space simply add. From this one rule comes harmony, noise cancellation, room resonance, speaker crossovers, and the Fourier Transform.

📖 Study
  • Constructive and destructive interference
  • Adding two sine waves — same frequency, different phases
  • Adding two sine waves of different frequencies
  • Beats: two close frequencies produce a throb at the difference frequency
🔧 Hands-On
  • Python: plot sin(2π·440·t) + sin(2π·444·t). Play it. Count the beat rate (~4 Hz).
  • Generate two tones near your measurement mic. View in Audacity.
  • Sum a wave with its inverted copy. What do you expect? Does it happen?
📝 Homework
  • Tune two tone generators to 440 Hz and 442 Hz. Listen for the 2 Hz beat. Try 440 and 450. Record observations.
  • Predict what 440 Hz + 880 Hz sounds like. Generate it. Were you right?
🎬 Watch
  • Veritasium: "The Key to Understanding Fourier Transforms"
  • Any tuning fork beat frequency demonstration on YouTube
Phase 1 · Weeks 6–12
The Nature of Sound
From vibrating air to numbers on a screen — and back.
🎯 Goal: Measure and describe any sound physically and mathematically📐 Math: Logarithms (tangent), basic wave equations
1.1 What a Sound Wave Actually Is

Sound is a travelling pressure variation. Air molecules don't travel with the wave — they push each other in a chain. This matters when you later think about room modes and microphone capsule physics.

📖 Study
  • Longitudinal vs transverse waves. Sound is longitudinal.
  • Compression and rarefaction — draw the air molecule view
  • Wave speed c = 343 m/s at 20°C. Why it changes with temperature.
  • The wave equation: c = f × λ. Learn to rearrange it.
🔧 Hands-On
  • Calculate wavelengths for 20 Hz, 1 kHz, and 20 kHz. Compare to your room dimensions.
  • Clap in a bathroom or stairwell. Listen for the flutter echo.
  • Record a hand clap near a wall with your measurement mic. Can you see the direct sound and reflection as separate events on the waveform?
📝 Homework
  • Speed of sound test: Stand ~34 m from a hard wall. Clap and time the echo. Does it match c = 343 m/s?
  • Wavelength table: For each octave from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, calculate the wavelength. Keep this — you'll refer to it throughout the curriculum.
📊 Measure
  • Open Audacity. Record a hand clap in your room. Zoom in and examine the waveform structure.
1.2 Decibels — The Language of Loudness

Decibels are logarithmic because human hearing is logarithmic — we perceive ratios, not differences. Doubling power only adds 3 dB. Doubling perceived loudness takes about 10 dB.

📖 Study
  • dB = 10 × log₁₀(P/P_ref) for power; 20 × log₁₀(A/A_ref) for amplitude
  • SPL scale: 0 dBSPL = threshold of hearing, 120 dB = pain threshold
  • Why adding two identical sources only gives +3 dB
🔧 Hands-On
  • Python: plot dB = 20*log10(x) for x from 0.001 to 10. Study the curve shape.
  • Use a free SPL meter app. Measure: quiet room, conversation, music at normal level, music loud.
📝 Homework
  • If one speaker produces 80 dB, what do 2, 4, 8, and 16 identical speakers produce? Work it out with the formula.
  • Create a personal dB reference sheet: "3 dB = double the ___", "10 dB = ___× louder to my ears"
🎬 Watch
  • Search: "What is a decibel?" — any video that shows the formula, not just intuition.
🔀 Math Tangent — Logarithms

log₁₀(100) = 2 because 10² = 100. Crucially, log turns multiplication into addition: log(A×B) = log(A) + log(B). Spend one session on Khan Academy's logarithm basics. Key values to memorise: log₁₀(2) ≈ 0.3, log₁₀(0.5) ≈ -0.3, log₁₀(1) = 0.

1.3 Measuring Your Room — RT60

RT60 is the time for a sound to decay by 60 dB after the source stops. It's the single most important measure of a room's acoustic character. Your measurement mic earns its keep here.

📖 Study
  • What reverb is physically (multiple delayed reflections)
  • RT60 definition and what "good" values look like for different rooms
  • The Sabine equation: RT60 = 0.161V / A (V = volume, A = total absorption)
  • How RT60 varies by frequency — never the same at bass vs treble
📊 Measure
  • Download REW (Room EQ Wizard) — free, industry standard.
  • Connect your mic and audio interface. Run the RT60 measurement.
  • Record RT60 at multiple frequencies. Save the waterfall plot.
📝 Homework
  • Using the Sabine equation and your room dimensions, estimate RT60 theoretically. Look up absorption coefficients for your materials. Compare to REW. Explain discrepancies.
  • Measure RT60 with doors open vs closed, and with a duvet in the room. Note changes at different frequencies.
🎬 Watch
  • Search: "Getting started with Room EQ Wizard" on YouTube.
Phase 2 · Weeks 13–22
The Mathematics of Waves
Every sound is a sum of sine waves. Prove it.
🎯 Goal: Understand and use the Fourier Transform. Analyse sounds in Python.📐 Math: Complex numbers, Euler's formula, introductory calculus
2.1 Complex Numbers — A Necessary Detour

Complex numbers let you represent both amplitude and phase in a single number. Every filter, every phasor diagram, every impedance calculation uses them.

📖 Study
  • j² = -1. Accept it and move on.
  • Complex number a + jb on the Argand diagram (a 2D plane)
  • Magnitude: |z| = √(a² + b²). Phase: θ = arctan(b/a)
  • Euler's formula: e^(jθ) = cos(θ) + j·sin(θ)
  • Multiplying complex numbers = rotating + scaling
🔧 Hands-On
  • Python: import cmath. Compute e^(j * π/2) and e^(j * π). What do you get?
  • Plot a complex number rotating around the unit circle as θ increases 0 → 2π. The real part is your cosine wave. The imaginary part is your sine wave.
📝 Homework
  • Your laser projector draws Lissajous figures — exactly the real vs imaginary parts of a complex exponential. Write a paragraph connecting this to what you now know.
  • Calculate magnitude and phase of: 3 + 4j, -1 + j, and 1 - j. Check with Python.
🎬 Watch
  • 3Blue1Brown: complex number playlist
  • Welch Labs: "Imaginary Numbers are Real" — 12-part series. 2 episodes per sitting.
🔀 Math Tangent — Just Enough Calculus

A derivative is the instantaneous slope at a point. An integral is the area under a curve. Key fact: the derivative of sin(t) is cos(t), and the integral of cos(t) is sin(t). In audio, this means velocity and pressure in a sound wave are 90° out of phase — exactly what complex numbers encode. Watch the first two 3Blue1Brown "Essence of Calculus" videos. That's enough for now.

2.2 The Fourier Series — Building Sounds from Sine Waves

Any periodic wave can be built from a sum of sine waves. A square wave. A sawtooth. A vowel. Your PWM square wave has harmonics — this is why.

📖 Study
  • Harmonics: integer multiples of a fundamental frequency (f, 2f, 3f…)
  • Fourier Series — each harmonic has an amplitude and phase
  • Why a square wave contains only odd harmonics (1f, 3f, 5f, 7f…)
  • Why instruments have characteristic timbres — it's their harmonic content
🔧 Hands-On
  • Python: build a square wave by summing first 1, 3, 5, 10, and 50 odd harmonics. Plot each. Watch it converge.
  • Record a musical instrument or vowel. Open in Audacity → Analyse → Plot Spectrum. Can you see the harmonics?
  • Arduino: generate a square wave. Compare its spectrum to a Python-generated pure square.
📝 Homework
  • Record three sounds: a whistle, a spoken vowel, and a clap. Screenshot their spectra. Write a paragraph on each — what does the harmonic structure reveal about the source?
  • Your PWM LED at 1 kHz produces a square wave. What harmonics does it contain? Why might this matter if you used PWM for audio? (This is how Class D amplifiers work.)
🎬 Watch
  • 3Blue1Brown: "But what is a Fourier series? From heat flow to drawing with circles." — watch it twice.
2.3 The FFT — Your Most Powerful Tool

The Fast Fourier Transform takes a time-domain signal and outputs its frequency spectrum. It's the engine behind REW, Audacity, every audio plugin, and most of modern communications.

📖 Study
  • Continuous vs Discrete Fourier Transform
  • FFT as an efficient algorithm for the DFT
  • Frequency resolution: Δf = sample_rate / N_samples
  • The concept of windowing — why you taper the signal before FFT
🔧 Hands-On
  • Python + numpy: generate 1 kHz + 3 kHz sine. Run np.fft.fft(). Plot magnitude. Two peaks.
  • Record yourself humming. FFT it. Identify fundamental and harmonics.
  • Experiment with window size (N = 256, 1024, 8192). See how frequency resolution changes.
  • Apply a Hanning window before FFT. Compare to without.
📝 Homework
  • Record 10 seconds of room noise. FFT it in Python. Identify any periodic components. (UK mains hum = 50 Hz and harmonics.)
  • Build a live spectrum analyser: Python + matplotlib animation reading from your audio interface in real time. This is a 1–2 week project. The process teaches you more than any textbook.
🎬 Watch
  • Computerphile: "The Fourier Transform"
  • Steve Brunton (University of Washington): FFT lecture on YouTube
Phase 3 · Weeks 23–30
Room Acoustics
Your room is an instrument. Learn to play it — and tune it.
🎯 Goal: Measure, understand, and improve a real room acoustically📐 Math: Standing wave equations, Sabine equation in depth
3.1 Room Modes and Standing Waves

When a sound wave reflects off a wall and meets itself, the waves interfere. At certain frequencies they set up standing waves — patterns of loud and quiet zones that don't move. Every rectangular room has a set of these "modes".

📖 Study
  • Axial, tangential, and oblique modes
  • Mode formula: f = (c/2)×√((l/L)²+(m/W)²+(n/H)²)
  • Schroeder frequency: transition between modal and diffuse behaviour (~200 Hz in most rooms)
  • Mode "stacking" — when multiple modes cluster near the same frequency
🔧 Hands-On
  • Calculate first three axial modes for your room using f = c/(2L) for each dimension.
  • REW: run frequency response. Overlay your calculated mode frequencies. Do the peaks match?
  • Play a sine sweep. Walk around slowly. Notice where bass disappears — those are mode nulls.
📝 Homework
  • Calculate all axial modes up to 300 Hz. Plot on a frequency axis. Identify frequencies where modes cluster — these will sound boomy.
  • Set a tone to your first axial mode. Place your mic at different positions. Log SPL. Draw a rough "mode map".
📊 Measure
  • Take 9 frequency response measurements at different room positions (corners, centre, listening position). Average them.
3.2 Absorption, Diffusion, and Treatment

Absorbers reduce energy (reducing RT60). Diffusers scatter reflections without removing energy. Bass traps target low-frequency modes. The right mix depends on your room and goals.

📖 Study
  • Absorption coefficient α: 0 = perfect reflector, 1 = perfect absorber
  • How porous absorbers work: friction converts sound to heat
  • Bass traps: why low frequencies need thick material (quarter-wavelength rule)
  • Diffuser design: QRD (Quadratic Residue Diffusers) and why prime numbers matter
🔧 Hands-On
  • Hang a duvet in a corner. Measure RT60 before and after. How much did it change at which frequencies?
  • Stack books/furniture in different corner positions. Remeasure. Develop intuition.
  • Use Amroc (online room mode calculator) to compare ideal dimension ratios to your actual room.
📝 Homework
  • Design (on paper) a set of acoustic treatment panels for one of your rooms. Show placement, dimensions, material, and predict the effect using Sabine. Don't build yet — design it properly first.
  • Find absorption coefficients for: 50mm Rockwool RW3, 100mm Rockwool RW3, carpet, glass, plasterboard. Build a table showing how they vary by frequency.
🎬 Watch
  • GIK Acoustics YouTube channel — practical room treatment guides
  • Ethan Winer: Acoustics 101 on YouTube
Phase 4 · Weeks 31–40
Electronics for Audio
Filters, impedance, and the circuits that shape sound before it becomes air.
🎯 Goal: Design and build passive and active audio filters. Understand crossovers.📐 Math: Complex impedance, Bode plots, transfer functions
4.1 Impedance and Reactance

In AC (audio) circuits, capacitors and inductors resist current in a frequency-dependent way. This is reactance. It's what makes filters possible. Complex numbers pay off here: impedance is a complex number.

📖 Study
  • Resistors: Z = R (real, frequency-independent)
  • Capacitors: Z = 1/(jωC) — impedance falls with frequency
  • Inductors: Z = jωL — impedance rises with frequency
  • ω = 2πf appears in every filter equation
  • Ohm's law in complex form: V = IZ
🔧 Hands-On
  • Calculate impedance of a 10 µF capacitor at 100 Hz, 1 kHz, 10 kHz. Notice the trend.
  • Inject a swept tone into an RC circuit and measure the output with your audio interface. Plot it.
  • Simulate in LTspice (free). Build an RC circuit. Run AC analysis.
📝 Homework
  • For a 1 µF capacitor and a 10 mH inductor, calculate impedance at 10, 100, 1k, 10k Hz. Note where capacitor impedance equals inductor impedance — this is resonance.
🎬 Watch
  • ElectroBOOM or EEVblog on AC circuits and reactive components
4.2 Passive Filters and Crossovers

A crossover splits audio — sending bass to a woofer, treble to a tweeter. It's one of the most elegant applications of filter theory. Build one on a kitchen table.

📖 Study
  • First-order RC low-pass: cutoff frequency f_c = 1/(2πRC)
  • High-pass filter: same components, different output tap
  • Second-order filters: -12dB/octave rolloff. Butterworth alignment.
  • Bode plots: log frequency vs dB gain. Draw them by hand.
  • Crossover types: 6dB, 12dB, 24dB per octave and their tradeoffs
🔧 Hands-On
  • Build a first-order low-pass filter on breadboard. Measure frequency response with your audio interface.
  • Design a 2-way passive crossover at 3 kHz for 8Ω speakers. Calculate L and C values. Simulate in LTspice.
  • Open a cheap bookshelf speaker. Identify the crossover components.
📝 Homework
  • Design a first-order crossover at 2.5 kHz for an 8Ω system. Calculate inductor (low-pass) and capacitor (high-pass) values. Show working.
  • Draw the Bode plot by hand. Rule: at f_c, gain = -3 dB. Below: flat. Above: -6 dB/octave. Verify with LTspice.
📊 Measure
  • Use REW to measure the frequency response of your built filter. Overlay the theoretical Bode plot. Explain differences.
4.3 Active Filters and Op-Amps

Passive filters can't amplify and load the signal source. Active filters using op-amps solve both. The Sallen-Key topology is the workhorse of audio filter design.

📖 Study
  • Op-amp basics: virtual ground, infinite input impedance, feedback
  • Inverting and non-inverting amplifier configurations
  • Sallen-Key low-pass filter topology
  • State-variable filters: simultaneous LP, HP, BP outputs
  • Q factor: how "peaky" a filter is at its cutoff
🔧 Hands-On
  • Simulate a Sallen-Key low-pass in LTspice. Vary Q from 0.5 to 2. Observe the response change.
  • Build an inverting op-amp amplifier on breadboard with a TL072. Measure the gain.
  • Build a Sallen-Key filter. Measure it. Compare to simulation.
📝 Homework
  • Research how a parametric EQ band is implemented using op-amps. Sketch the circuit and explain how frequency, gain, and Q are each controlled.
🎬 Watch
  • W2AEW on YouTube: practical op-amp filter design videos
  • Search: "Sallen-Key filter design tutorial"
Phase 5 · Weeks 41–52
Digital Signal Processing
Where maths, code, and sound become the same thing.
🎯 Goal: Write working DSP algorithms in Python and on Arduino/Teensy📐 Math: Sampling theory, convolution, z-transform (conceptual)
5.1 Sampling, Nyquist, and Digital Audio

Your audio interface takes 44,100 samples per second. The Nyquist theorem says this represents any frequency up to 22,050 Hz. Violate this and you get aliasing — phantom frequencies.

📖 Study
  • Sampling theorem: f_sample ≥ 2 × f_max
  • Aliasing: what happens when you undersample
  • Quantisation: amplitude discretisation and bit depth (16-bit = 96 dB dynamic range)
  • Anti-aliasing filters: why your ADC has a low-pass filter before the sampler
🔧 Hands-On
  • Python: generate a 1 kHz sine at 44100 Hz sample rate. Downsample to 2200 Hz. Listen to the aliased result.
  • Demonstrate aliasing visually: sample a 0.9× and 1.1× Nyquist sine at the same rate. Show they produce identical samples.
📝 Homework
  • Requantise a recording to 8, 4, and 2 bits in Python. Listen to each. Describe the degradation.
  • Arduino's ADC is 10-bit at ~9600 samples/sec. What's the max audio frequency? How does quality compare to CD? (This is why Teensy is preferred for audio.)
🎬 Watch
  • Monty Montgomery (Xiph.org): "A Digital Media Primer for Geeks" — the definitive visual explanation. Required.
5.2 Convolution and the Impulse Response

The impulse response of a room contains everything about how it processes sound. Convolve any audio with that IR and you've put it inside that room — virtually. This is how reverb plugins work.

📖 Study
  • Convolution definition: (f * g)[n] = Σ f[k] · g[n-k]
  • Convolution in frequency domain = multiplication (why FFT makes convolution fast)
  • Impulse response: system output to a single sample spike
  • LTI systems — why audio circuits and rooms qualify
🔧 Hands-On
  • Record an IR of your room: balloon pop or REW's sine sweep method.
  • Python: convolve a dry voice with your room IR using scipy.signal.convolve(). Play the result.
  • Download IRs from openairlib.net (free). Apply St Paul's Cathedral IR to a dry guitar. Acoustic teleportation.
📝 Homework
  • Your laser traces paths over time — a 2D signal. Convolving the X/Y drive signals with a short IR would change the projected shape. Write a paragraph on this analogy.
  • Build a convolution reverb script: takes any audio file + any IR file as inputs, outputs reverb-processed audio. Make it a proper CLI tool with argparse.
🎬 Watch
  • 3Blue1Brown: "But what is a convolution?"
  • Search: "Convolution reverb explained"
5.3 Digital Filters — FIR and IIR

FIR filters are linear phase and always stable. IIR filters are efficient but can ring. Both have their place. The biquad is the standard IIR building block in audio DSP.

📖 Study
  • FIR: output = weighted sum of recent input samples
  • IIR: includes feedback of previous outputs
  • Biquad filter: 5 coefficients b0, b1, b2, a1, a2
  • Audio EQ Cookbook (Robert Bristow-Johnson) — the definitive free reference
🔧 Hands-On
  • Python: implement an FIR low-pass using numpy.convolve with a manual kernel. Measure its response with FFT.
  • Use scipy.signal.butter() to design a Butterworth IIR. Compare FIR vs IIR on the same signal.
  • Implement a biquad peaking EQ using the Audio EQ Cookbook. Apply +6 dB at 1 kHz, Q=2. Verify with spectrum analysis.
📝 Homework
  • Arduino DSP: implement a simple IIR low-pass on Arduino or Teensy. Sample audio, apply the difference equation, output via PWM/DAC. This is the practical culmination of Phases 4 and 5.
  • Build a Python parametric EQ tool: applies multiple biquad bands from a text config file. "100 Hz, -3 dB, Q=1.5 / 1000 Hz, +2 dB, Q=2"
🎬 Watch
  • Hack Audio (Eric Tarr) — YouTube, clear DSP tutorials
  • Julius O. Smith III: free DSP textbook at ccrma.stanford.edu
5.4 Room Correction DSP

The payoff: using all your measurement and DSP knowledge to automatically correct a room's frequency response. This is what Dirac Live and Audyssey do in high-end AV receivers.

📖 Study
  • Minimum phase vs linear phase correction
  • FIR correction filters: inverting the room's frequency response
  • Why you can't correct everything (modes respond differently per position)
  • Target curves: why flat in-room isn't ideal (Harman target curve)
🔧 Hands-On
  • REW: use the EQ feature to generate correction filters. Export and apply to your playback.
  • Python: take your room's measured frequency response, invert it, design a correction FIR filter, convolve with audio. Measure before/after.
📝 Homework
  • Before/after report: document your room before and after applying REW's EQ. Include frequency response plots, RT60 plots, and subjective listening notes. This becomes the foundation for your capstone.
📊 Measure
  • Compare your measured response to the Harman target curve in REW. Which deviations are correctable by EQ and which require physical treatment?
Capstone · Weeks 53+
Design, Build, Measure, Correct
Bring everything together into one real, finished system.
🎯 Goal: A complete, measured, corrected audio system designed from first principles

The capstone is not a test — it's a demonstration to yourself that the knowledge is real. Design and deploy a complete room-corrected audio system, documented with measurements, theory, and reflection.

1

Acoustic audit. Full REW measurement suite: frequency response (multiple positions), RT60, waterfall, room modes. Document everything with theory explanations.

2

Treatment design. Using Sabine's equation and your absorption data, design a physical treatment plan. Calculate predicted RT60 improvement. Build at least one panel.

3

Crossover design. Choose a 2-way speaker system. Design a crossover network (active or passive). Simulate in LTspice. Build it if budget allows.

4

DSP correction. Design a room correction FIR filter in Python using your REW measurements. Implement as a Python audio processing pipeline.

5

Arduino/Teensy DSP. Implement a basic parametric EQ on a Teensy 4.0 using the Teensy Audio Library. This closes the loop back to your roots.

6

Final measurements and report. Full before/after comparison. Write up the physics behind every decision. This document is your proof of mastery.

💡 Optional stretch: Add an audio-reactive mode to your flying laser projector — FFT of live microphone input drives the mirror patterns. Low frequencies control one axis, high frequencies the other. This merges everything in this curriculum into a single visual-acoustic instrument.

📌 A Note on Pacing

The timelines here are guides, not deadlines. Some modules will click in a day; others might need three times the suggested time. That's completely normal. If you hit a wall, step back, redo the homework differently, or take the maths tangent before continuing.

Come back to this chat when you're stuck, when something unexpected happens in a measurement, or when you want to go deeper. Every phase here is a starting point, not an ending point.
